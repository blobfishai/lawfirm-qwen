# legalbench-rule-application pack — Firm rule memo: Abercrombie spectrum of trademark distinctiveness

WHITLOCK & DEENE LLP — INTERNAL RULE MEMORANDUM (Brands & Trademark Group)
Re: Classifying marks on the Abercrombie spectrum during clearance

Section 1. The five categories. Every candidate mark is classified into exactly one of five categories, listed from weakest to strongest:
(1) generic — the mark is the common name for the product or service itself. The word answers the question 'what is it?' (e.g., the word for the product category used as the brand of that very product). Generic terms receive no protection.
(2) descriptive — the mark directly describes a purpose, nature, attribute, feature, quality, or characteristic of the product. No mental leap is required: the consumer reads the mark and immediately understands, without imagination, a fact about the product. Descriptive marks are protectable only with secondary meaning.
(3) suggestive — the mark suggests or implies a quality or characteristic, requiring the consumer to exercise imagination, thought, or a multi-step inference to connect the mark to the product's attributes. The dividing line from descriptive: if the mark states the attribute directly, it is descriptive; if the consumer must reason through an association to reach the attribute, it is suggestive.
(4) arbitrary — the mark is a real, existing word (a word found in the dictionary or in ordinary usage) that has NO relation to the product it brands.
(5) fanciful — the mark is an invented or coined word: a term with no prior meaning in any language, created for the purpose of serving as a mark.

Section 2. Firm classification discipline. Apply the categories in this order of inquiry. First ask whether the term exists as a word at all: an invented term with no dictionary meaning is fanciful — it can never be arbitrary, because arbitrary status is reserved for real words. If the term is a real word or a readable combination of real words, ask what it communicates about the product: the common name is generic; a direct statement of an attribute the product actually has is descriptive; an indirect implication requiring imagination is suggestive; no relation at all is arbitrary.

Section 3. Recording. Clearance determinations are logged as evidence records on the client matter, one record per mark, with the category slug in lowercase exactly as written in Section 1: generic, descriptive, suggestive, arbitrary, fanciful. The assignment instructions state where each record is filed. When the client's letter recites why a name was chosen and what the product's actual features are, treat those recitals as established facts for classification purposes.
