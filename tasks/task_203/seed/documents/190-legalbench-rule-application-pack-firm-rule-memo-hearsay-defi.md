# legalbench-rule-application pack — Firm rule memo: Hearsay definition and assertive conduct (FRE 801)

WHITLOCK & DEENE LLP — INTERNAL RULE MEMORANDUM (Evidence Practice Group)
Re: Applying the hearsay definition in trial preparation

Section 1. The rule. Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the statement. Both elements are required: (a) the statement must have been made outside the current trial or hearing, and (b) the proponent must be offering it to prove that its content is true. If either element is missing, the item is not hearsay and this memo's test is not satisfied.

Section 2. What counts as a 'statement.' A statement is (i) an oral assertion, (ii) a written assertion, or (iii) nonverbal conduct of a person, if the person intended the conduct as an assertion. The firm's settled position, which associates must apply without deviation: a nod, a headshake, or pointing in direct response to a question is nonverbal conduct intended as an assertion and is therefore a statement. If such conduct occurred out of court and is offered to prove the truth of what it asserted, it IS hearsay. By contrast, conduct not intended to communicate anything — for example, a person putting up an umbrella, or an athlete's record time in a race — is not a statement at all and can never be hearsay.

Section 3. Common non-hearsay purposes. An out-of-court statement is NOT hearsay when offered for a purpose other than its truth, including: (a) effect on the listener — to show that the hearer was on notice, was warned, or had a motive, regardless of whether the statement was accurate; (b) verbal acts — words with independent legal significance, such as the words of offer, acceptance, or defamation; (c) impeachment — to show a witness said something inconsistent, not that the earlier statement was true; (d) circumstantial evidence of the declarant's state of mind. Live, in-court testimony given by a witness on the stand at the current proceeding is likewise not hearsay, even if elicited on cross-examination.

Section 4. Application discipline. Identify (1) the declarant, (2) where the statement was made, and (3) the precise purpose stated by the proponent. When our client letter states the purpose for which an item will be offered, take that stated purpose as controlling; do not invent an alternative purpose. If the stated purpose is to prove the truth of the matter asserted and the statement was made out of court, the hearsay test is satisfied. If the stated purpose is notice or another listed non-truth purpose, the test is not satisfied even for an identical statement.
